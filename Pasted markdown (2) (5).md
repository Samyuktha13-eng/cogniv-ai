# LAKSHMI — THE MEMORIES OF A LIFETIME

## 1. The Jasmine Morning

Every morning, before the rest of the house woke up, Lakshmi would open the wooden back door.

She would step outside barefoot.

The courtyard would still be cool from the night.

She kept a small brass pot beside the door.

She would pick it up, walk to the tap, turn the handle slowly so the water wouldn't splash onto her sari, and fill the pot only halfway.

She always filled it halfway.

Her daughter used to ask her,

"Why don't you fill it completely?"

Lakshmi would laugh.

"Because then I'll have to carry all that water."

She would carry the pot with both hands and walk toward the jasmine plant.

Before watering it, she would first look at the flowers.

She never plucked the flowers that were still closed.

She would wait for the ones that had opened during the night.

She would bend down, hold the stem between two fingers, and gently twist each flower away.

One flower.

Then another.

Then another.

She would put them into the end of her sari, which she folded upward to make a little pouch.

When she had collected enough, she would sit on the small stone beside the plant.

She would separate the flowers from the leaves.

Then she would take a long white thread from the little metal box she kept near the window.

She would wet the thread slightly between her fingers.

She said it made the thread easier to handle.

Then she would push each jasmine flower onto the needle.

She never rushed.

Flower after flower.

She would make a long string of jasmine.

When she finished, she would tie the two ends together.

One garland was for the small prayer room.

One was for her daughter's hair.

And if there were extra flowers, she would put them in a steel bowl filled with water.

The entire house would smell of jasmine.

That smell became the smell of morning.

---

# 2. The Mango Tree

During summer holidays, Lakshmi would wake before her father.

She knew exactly where he kept the mangoes.

He would bring them home in a large cloth bag and leave the bag on the kitchen floor.

Lakshmi would wait until he went outside.

Then she would quietly enter the kitchen.

She would open the bag.

She would inspect every mango.

She always chose the largest one.

She would press it gently with her thumb.

If it was soft, she knew it was ready.

She would take it outside.

But she never ate it immediately.

She would run toward the mango tree behind the house.

First, she would put one foot against the lowest part of the trunk.

Then she would hold the branch above her head.

She would pull herself up.

She had a particular branch where she liked to sit.

She would sit there with the mango in her lap.

Then she would wipe the mango against the edge of her dress.

She would bite into the skin.

Juice would run down her wrist.

She would lick it away.

Sometimes her father would find her.

"Lakshmi!"

She would freeze.

"I told you not to climb that tree!"

She would quickly hide the mango behind her back.

Her father would pretend to be angry.

Then he would climb up after her.

He would take the mango from her.

He would cut it with his small pocket knife.

He would sprinkle salt and chilli powder over the pieces.

Then he would give her the largest piece.

Lakshmi always believed he didn't notice that she had already eaten half of it.

---

# 3. The Rainy-Day Kitchen

When the monsoon arrived, Lakshmi's mother would change the entire rhythm of the house.

The windows would be closed halfway.

The clothes would be brought inside.

The floor would smell of wet earth.

Lakshmi would sit near the kitchen door and watch the rain.

Her mother would place a large iron pan on the stove.

First came the oil.

Then mustard seeds.

Lakshmi loved listening for the moment they began to crackle.

Her mother would throw curry leaves into the hot oil.

They would make a loud sizzling sound.

Lakshmi would immediately move closer.

"Don't come so close," her mother would say.

Lakshmi would move back.

Then, when her mother wasn't looking, she would move forward again.

Her mother would cut onions.

Lakshmi would peel garlic.

She would take one clove at a time, place it on the wooden board, press it with the flat side of the knife, and remove the skin.

Sometimes she would crush more garlic than necessary just because she liked the smell.

When the food was ready, her mother would place a little portion into a small steel bowl.

She would blow on it to cool it.

Then she would give it to Lakshmi.

Lakshmi would taste it.

She always knew whether more salt was needed.

Her mother would pretend not to listen.

Then she would add exactly what Lakshmi suggested.

---

# 4. The School Morning

When Lakshmi was twelve, she walked to school every morning with Radha.

They never walked directly.

They had their own route.

First they passed the old temple.

Then they crossed the small bridge.

Then they stopped beside the tea shop.

The tea shop owner knew them.

He would keep two small pieces of jaggery on the counter.

They would each take one.

Then they continued walking.

Lakshmi carried her books in a blue cloth bag.

Radha carried hers in a brown bag.

Halfway to school, Lakshmi would remove her shoes.

She liked walking barefoot on the soft dirt.

When they reached the school gate, she would put them back on.

Inside the classroom, she always sat near the window.

She would place her notebook on the desk.

Open the pencil box.

Sharpen her pencil.

Put the pencil shavings into the corner of the desk.

She never threw them on the floor.

During mathematics class, she would draw tiny flowers in the last page of her notebook.

Whenever the teacher turned toward the blackboard, Radha would lean over and whisper something.

Lakshmi would try not to laugh.

Sometimes they would both get scolded.

After school, they would sit beneath the neem tree and share their lunch.

Radha always had lemon rice.

Lakshmi always had curd rice.

They would exchange one spoonful.

They did this every day.

Neither of them ever called it a tradition.

It simply became one.

---

# 5. The Railway Station

Years later, Lakshmi met the man who would become her husband at a railway station.

It was raining heavily.

She was standing beneath the station roof, holding her books against her chest.

Her sandal strap had broken.

She tried to fix it.

It wouldn't hold.

She became frustrated.

Then someone crouched beside her.

A young man was holding a small brown suitcase.

He took a piece of string from his bag.

He tied the sandal carefully.

Lakshmi looked at him.

"Thank you."

He stood up.

"Don't walk too fast. It will come loose."

She looked down.

Then she noticed that one of her books had fallen.

He picked it up.

He looked at the cover.

"You like reading?"

She nodded.

"What kind?"

She told him.

The station announcement interrupted them.

His train had arrived.

He picked up his suitcase.

Before leaving, he turned around.

"Keep reading."

Then he disappeared into the crowd.

Lakshmi watched the train leave.

She didn't know it then.

But she would meet him again.

---

# 6. Her Wedding Morning

On her wedding day, Lakshmi woke before everyone else.

She couldn't sleep.

She sat on the edge of the bed and listened to people moving around the house.

Someone was boiling milk.

Someone was washing vessels.

Someone was calling for flowers.

Someone was looking for a missing safety pin.

Lakshmi opened the wooden box containing her jewelry.

She took out her bangles.

One by one, she placed them on her wrist.

Then she wore her earrings.

Her mother entered carrying the red sari.

Lakshmi touched the fabric.

It was heavier than the saris she usually wore.

Her mother helped her drape it.

She adjusted the pleats.

Then she fixed the jasmine flowers into Lakshmi's hair.

Lakshmi looked at herself in the mirror.

She didn't recognize the woman looking back.

She looked older.

Different.

Ready.

When she walked outside, her father stood near the doorway.

He looked at her.

For a few seconds, he said nothing.

Then he smiled.

"You're still my little girl."

Lakshmi began crying.

---

# 7. The First Home With Her Husband

After marriage, Lakshmi and her husband lived in a small rented house.

The kitchen was tiny.

There was barely enough space for two people to stand.

But Lakshmi loved that kitchen.

She would wake early.

She would open the window.

She would sweep the floor.

She would wash the previous night's vessels.

Then she would put rice on the stove.

Her husband would sit outside reading the newspaper.

Every morning he folded the newspaper in exactly the same way.

Lakshmi would bring him tea.

He would take one sip.

Then he would say,

"Too hot."

She would reply,

"Then wait."

Five minutes later he would finish the entire cup.

When he left for work, she would stand at the doorway.

He would turn around before reaching the street.

He always turned around.

She always waved.

---

# 8. The Day Her Daughter Was Born

When her daughter was born, Lakshmi remembered almost every detail.

The hospital corridor.

The white walls.

The smell of disinfectant.

The sound of footsteps.

Her husband's nervous pacing.

The doctor finally coming out.

"It's a girl."

Lakshmi was exhausted.

But when they placed the baby beside her, she opened her eyes.

The baby was tiny.

Her fingers curled around Lakshmi's little finger.

Lakshmi didn't move.

She was afraid that if she moved, the baby would disappear.

She counted the baby's fingers.

One.

Two.

Three.

Four.

Five.

She counted them again.

That night, she couldn't sleep.

She kept looking at the baby.

Every few minutes she would check whether she was breathing.

Then she would touch her forehead.

Then pull the blanket up.

Then check again.

That was the first night Lakshmi became a mother.

---

# 9. The First Day of School

Years later, Lakshmi's daughter started school.

Lakshmi woke before her.

She ironed the uniform.

She polished the shoes.

She packed the lunch.

She placed two pencils inside the pencil box.

Then she placed one extra pencil.

She always packed an extra.

She braided her daughter's hair.

First the left side.

Then the right.

Then she tied two ribbons.

Her daughter stood in front of the mirror.

"I don't want to go."

Lakshmi knelt down.

"You'll be back before you know it."

At the school gate, her daughter held her hand tightly.

When the teacher came, the little girl refused to let go.

Lakshmi gently removed her fingers one by one.

"Go."

Her daughter walked inside.

Lakshmi stayed outside the gate.

She watched through the bars.

She didn't leave until her daughter disappeared into the classroom.

Then she walked home alone.

Halfway home, she realized she was crying.

---

# 10. The First Festival With Her Children

Every festival, Lakshmi woke before sunrise.

She cleaned the front entrance.

She washed the floor.

She mixed rice flour with water.

Then she sat on the ground and began drawing a rangoli.

First she placed the dots.

One row.

Then another.

Then another.

She connected them carefully.

A curve here.

A circle there.

A flower in the middle.

Her daughter would sit beside her and try to copy it.

The lines were never straight.

Lakshmi never corrected her.

"Keep going," she would say.

By the time they finished, both of them would have flour on their fingers.

Then Lakshmi would stand up.

She would prepare the food.

Arrange the lamps.

Change into a fresh sari.

Put jasmine flowers in her hair.

Light the first lamp.

Then she would call everyone.

"Come. It's ready."

The house would become noisy.

Children running.

People talking.

Vessels clattering.

Music playing.

Someone laughing.

Someone calling from another room.

Lakshmi would stand in the middle of it all.

That was her favorite sound.

Her family being alive around her.

---

# 11. Her Husband's Evening Ritual

Every evening, her husband returned home at almost the same time.

Lakshmi could hear his footsteps before he opened the door.

He would remove his shoes outside.

Put his keys on the small wooden shelf.

Wash his hands.

Then sit in the same chair.

Lakshmi would bring him tea.

He would ask,

"What happened today?"

She would tell him everything.

What the children did.

What the neighbor said.

What vegetable was expensive.

Which flower bloomed.

What recipe she tried.

He would listen.

Sometimes he wasn't really listening.

But he always nodded.

Before sleeping, he would check the doors.

First the front door.

Then the back door.

Then the windows.

Lakshmi would wait in bed.

When he returned, she knew the house was safe.

---

# 12. The Last Letter

Years later, her husband became sick.

Lakshmi became his caregiver.

She woke him.

Helped him walk.

Prepared his medicines.

Cooked softer food.

Sat beside him.

Sometimes he couldn't remember things either.

But he remembered her.

One evening, while sitting beside the window, he held her hand.

He said,

"You've taken care of everyone."

Lakshmi smiled.

"Someone had to."

He laughed.

Before he died, he gave her a folded letter.

She kept it inside the brass box.

She never opened it immediately.

She waited.

Years later, when she finally opened it, there were only a few words.

She read them again and again.

Then folded the paper.

Placed it back inside the box.

And closed the lid.

---

# 13. The Grandchildren

When her grandchildren were born, Lakshmi changed again.

She became the person who allowed everything.

She allowed them to eat mangoes before lunch.

She allowed them to play in the rain.

She allowed them to touch her jewelry.

She allowed them to sleep beside her.

She told them stories at night.

She would lie between them.

One child would hold her left arm.

Another would hold her right.

She would begin a story.

"Once there was a little girl..."

Halfway through, one child would interrupt.

"What happened next?"

Lakshmi would pretend to be angry.

"I was getting there!"

Then she would continue.

She told the same stories so many times that her grandchildren knew the endings.

But they still asked.

Because they didn't want a new story.

They wanted **her story**.

---

# 14. The House After Everyone Left

Eventually, the children grew up.

The house became quieter.

The toys disappeared.

The school bags disappeared.

The extra shoes disappeared.

The dining table had fewer plates.

Lakshmi began keeping photographs everywhere.

One on the television.

One near the bed.

One in the kitchen.

One inside the brass box.

Sometimes she would take them out.

Arrange them in chronological order.

Childhood.

Wedding.

Children.

School.

Festivals.

Grandchildren.

She would look at every photograph.

Sometimes she would laugh.

Sometimes she would cry.

Sometimes she would put everything back.

---

# 15. The Memory of Making Pickles

Every summer, Lakshmi made mango pickle.

She would wash the mangoes.

Dry them completely.

She was very particular about this.

"No water," she would say.

She would wipe each mango with a clean cloth.

Then she would cut them into pieces.

Place them in a large steel bowl.

Add salt.

Red chilli powder.

Mustard.

Oil.

She would mix everything with her hands.

Then she would fill a large glass jar.

She would close the lid tightly.

The jar would sit near the kitchen window.

Every morning for several days, Lakshmi would open it.

Stir the pickle.

Close it again.

She never allowed anyone else to touch it.

"You're going to spoil it."

But when it was ready, she would proudly serve it at lunch.

---

# 16. The Last Walk Through the Garden

Many years later, Lakshmi walked through the garden again.

She walked slowly.

She touched the jasmine plant.

Then the mango tree.

Then the old stone beside the wall.

She ran her fingers across the rough bark.

She remembered sitting there.

She remembered her father.

She remembered her children.

She remembered her husband.

She remembered her grandchildren.

She remembered the girl she had once been.

The mother she became.

The wife she became.

The grandmother she became.

Her life had happened in this garden.

Not in one moment.

But in thousands of tiny actions.

Plucking flowers.

Climbing trees.

Cooking food.

Washing clothes.

Packing lunches.

Braiding hair.

Waiting at railway stations.

Holding babies.

Writing letters.

Making pickles.

Lighting lamps.

Waiting at doors.

And loving people.

Those were Lakshmi's memories.

Not simply **"I went to the garden."**

But:

**I opened the door.**
**I walked barefoot.**
**I carried the brass pot.**
**I touched the jasmine leaves.**
**I chose only the flowers that had opened.**
**I collected them in my sari.**
**I sat beneath the tree.**
**I threaded them one by one.**
**I put the finished garland in my daughter's hair.**
**And then I went inside and began making breakfast.**

That was the life Lakshmi had lived.

A lifetime wasn't made from big events alone.

It was made from **thousands of little things she did every day.**

And those little things were the memories that made Lakshmi, Lakshmi.
